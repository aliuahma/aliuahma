Deep learning is now the foundation of all machine learning and AI, making it more important than ever for us to obtain an intimate understanding of the empirical phenomena and experimental skills involved in deep neural networks. I make the claim that we should use deep learning to learn deep learning—in particular, reliably predicting the outcome of experiments leads to understandingIlya Sutskever famously said, "Predicting the next token well means that you understand the underlying reality that led to the creation of that token.". Understanding requires interaction and mastery, prediction forces you to build your own 'world model'.

In this blog, we will systematically tackle various aspects of training deep neural networks, pose questions, design experiments, and analyze the results. As we go, I will ask you to make predictions about the outcome of experiments, and we will compare your predictions to the actual results. For each experiment, we will review relevant literature to understand the problem and design the experiment.

We will focus on pre-trainingPre-training is easier to reason about since all training starts from scratch, and findings from small scale can be extrapolated to large scales.. I will assume some familiarity with the basics of deep learning, as I will not spend much time reviewing basics.

There are four main components when specifying a deep learning algorithm:

  • Data specifies a distribution over inputs to the model (e.g. internet text)
  • Architecture specifies how theta maps to a model prediction (e.g. transformer produces log likelihoods)
  • Objective specifies error of theta via the loss function (e.g. cross-entropy loss on predicted likelihoods)
  • Optimization specifies how the model should update itself based on its loss (e.g. gradient descent on cross-entropy loss)
